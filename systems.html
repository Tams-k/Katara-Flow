Learning from what already works

Public reporting tools exist already. We studied three of them to see what to copy and what gap is left for Katara Flow to fill.

Three reporting systems compared
System What it does well The gap What Katara Flow does
FixMyStreet (UK) Map-based. Reports go to the right authority and are public, which reduces duplicates and pushes councils to respond. Resolution is checked by a follow-up questionnaire weeks later, so it depends on the reporter replying. NCWSC confirms resolution inside the system.
Kenya Power MyPower app and USSD Every complaint gets a reference number and is tracked until closed. USSD works without internet. Tracking is private to each customer, with no public view of all reports. Uses the same reference-and-track idea for drainage, with a light interface for poor connections.
Ushahidi Open-source crowdsourcing. Reports arrive by many channels and appear on a live map. Crowdsourced data can be poor or misleading, and it struggles under heavy traffic and with weak connectivity. Reports go to one accountable body, NCWSC, which updates every status.

What we took from each

  • From FixMyStreet

    Public reporting can change how authorities behave. Making progress visible builds accountability.

  • From Kenya Power

    A report-and-track model with reference numbers is already familiar to Kenyans, and use of it keeps growing.

  • From Ushahidi

    Seeing where problems happen on a map is powerful, but data needs a responsible owner to stay reliable.

At a glance

  • Every report moves through three stages: Reported, In progress and Resolved.
  • Katara Flow covers drainage problems in Nairobi only. It does not cover roads, waste collection or electricity.
  • Built to be light, so it works on a slow connection. Report a drain.
